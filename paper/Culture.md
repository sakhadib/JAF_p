# Cultures of Bangladesh: Bengali and Eleven Indigenous Ethnic Communities

## Executive overview

Bangladesh is often represented through its Bengali-speaking majority, but its cultural landscape also includes dozens of smaller ethnic communities whose languages, kinship systems, religions, ecological knowledge, artistic forms, and historical experiences differ substantially. The 2022 Population and Housing Census counted 1,650,478 people in the official â€œethnic populationâ€ category, about 1% of the national population; researchers and Indigenous organizations dispute the completeness of that count and use higher estimates. The communities examined here occupy three broad cultural-geographic zones: the Chittagong Hill Tracts (Chakma, Marma, Mro, and many Tripura); the northern and north-central plains (Santal, Oraon, Garo/Mandi, and Hajong); and the northeastern and southern border/coastal zones (Khasi, Manipuri/Meitei, and Rakhine).[^1][^2]

No community described below is culturally uniform. Religious denomination, village, clan, gender, generation, education, migration, urbanization, and inter-community contact all produce internal differences. Terms such as â€œtraditionalâ€ therefore describe historically documented patterns rather than rules followed unchanged by every present-day person.

## Terminology and scope

The terminology surrounding Bangladeshâ€™s smaller ethnic peoples is politically contested. Article 23A of the Constitution speaks of protecting and developing the cultures of â€œtribes, minor races, ethnic sects and communities,â€ while many community organizations prefer â€œIndigenous Peoplesâ€ or *Adivasi*; this difference is tied to debates over self-identification, customary institutions, collective land rights, and political autonomy. This report uses each communityâ€™s own name where possible and otherwise uses **Indigenous community** or **ethnic community**, without implying that every individual adopts the same political terminology.[^3]

â€œBengaliâ€ is an ethnolinguistic identity, whereas â€œBangladeshiâ€ is a citizenship identity. Bangladeshâ€™s Chakma, Marma, Santal, Garo, Khasi, Manipuri, Mro, Tripura, Oraon, Hajong, Rakhine, and other peoples are Bangladeshi citizens without thereby becoming ethnically Bengali. Conversely, Bengali culture itself is internally pluralâ€”Muslim, Hindu, Buddhist, Christian, secular, rural, urban, regional, and diasporicâ€”and emerged through long interaction among peoples and religious traditions.[^4]

Some older reference works use now-discredited racial typologies or present legendary migrations as settled history. This report does not repeat those classifications as scientific fact. Oral histories and origin traditions are culturally important, but they are distinguished here from conclusions securely established by historical or linguistic evidence.

## Bengali culture

### Formation and identity

Bengali culture is the cultural field of Bangla-speaking people across historical Bengal, here treated specifically in its Bangladeshi setting. It developed through the cumulative interaction of older regional traditions with Buddhist, Hindu, Islamic, Persianate, Mughal, colonial-European, and modern global influences. Bangla itself demonstrates this history through inherited Indo-Aryan structures and substantial Perso-Arabic and English vocabulary.[^4]

Bangladeshâ€™s Bengali identity is unusually language-centered. The Language Movement and the memory of 21 February connect language with political dignity, education, and national belonging; UNESCOâ€™s International Mother Language Day grew from Bangladeshâ€™s campaign for language rights. Yet Bangla has many regional speech formsâ€”Dhakaiya, Chattogram varieties, Sylheti, Noakhali varieties, Rangpuri and othersâ€”whose relationship to a standardized literary language can involve class and identity distinctions.[^13]

### Religion and social life

Most Bangladeshi Bengalis are Muslim, with a large Hindu minority and smaller Buddhist and Christian populations. Religious calendars organize family and public life through Eid al-Fitr, Eid al-Adha, Muharram, Durga Puja, Kali Puja, Saraswati Puja, Buddha Purnima, Christmas, and numerous local observances; Pohela Baishakh operates as a major cross-religious civic and seasonal festival. Bengali folk practice has historically permitted considerable overlap among formal religion, shrine traditions, agrarian rites, village deities, Sufi devotion, Vaishnava traditions, and local ideas about healing and protection.[^14]

Family life has traditionally emphasized kin obligation, hospitality, respect for elders, marriage alliances, and collective participation in births, weddings, funerals, and religious meals. Nuclear households have expanded with urbanization and migration, but extended-family networks remain important for care, finance, employment, inheritance, and ritual responsibility.

### Language, literature, and thought

Bangla literature extends from the Buddhist *Charyapada* tradition through medieval devotional, courtly, and narrative poetry to nineteenth-century print modernity and contemporary fiction, poetry, theatre, essays, and digital media. Its canonical field includes both sides of Bengal but has distinct Bangladeshi trajectories shaped by the Language Movement, Partition, the 1971 Liberation War, rural transformation, Islamic and secular thought, and debates about gender and class.[^4]

Oral traditions remain equally important: folktales, proverbs, riddles, *pala*, *puthi*, *Mymensingh Gitika*, work songs, wedding songs, lullabies, and region-specific narrative performance. Baul music combines poetry, music, itinerant performance, and an embodied spiritual philosophy that resists rigid religious and caste classification; UNESCO recognizes Baul songs as intangible cultural heritage.[^15][^16]

### Music, performance, and visual arts

Bengali musical life includes Rabindra Sangeet, Nazrul Sangeet, modern song, patriotic song, Islamic devotional forms, and regional folk genres such as Baul, Bhatiali, Bhawaiya, Jari, Sari, Murshidi, Gambhira, and Kabigan. Rivers, boats, separation, spiritual longing, agrarian labor, and seasonal change supply recurring images. Theatre ranges from *jatra* and village performance to modern group theatre; film and television have added nationally shared visual vocabularies.

Craft traditions include Jamdani and Tangail sari weaving, Nakshi Kantha embroidery, pottery, brass and bell-metal work, bamboo and cane craft, woodwork, rickshaw painting, folk scrolls, and alpana floor designs. UNESCOâ€™s Bangladesh list includes Baul songs, Jamdani weaving, Mangal Shobhajatra, and Tangail sari weaving, demonstrating the linkage between craft, festival, and collective identity.[^16]

### Food and dress

Rice is the staple around which fish, lentils, vegetables, leafy greens, meat, eggs, pickles, and mashed *bhorta* preparations are organized. Riverine ecology made fish culturally central, while mustard oil, green chilli, onion, garlic, turmeric, and regional spice combinations shape flavor. Pithaâ€”made from rice or wheat flour, coconut, molasses, milk, meat, or vegetablesâ€”is strongly associated with winter, harvests, hospitality, marriage, and family gathering.[^17]

Dress varies by religion, class, region, and setting. Saris, salwar-kameez, panjabi, lungi, dhoti, shirts, and trousers coexist, while Jamdani, Tangail textiles, muslin heritage, and embroidered fabrics carry special cultural prestige. Urban clothing is globally influenced, but festival dress remains a visible medium of Bengali identity.

### Calendar and heritage

Pohela Baishakh marks the Bengali New Year with fairs, music, food, commercial account-opening traditions, and civic processions. Mangal Shobhajatra, developed at the Faculty of Fine Art in Dhaka, uses large masks and symbolic figures as a public expression of solidarity and resistance and is UNESCO-listed. Nabanna celebrates new rice, while regional fairs connect trade, shrine visitation, performance, craft, courtship, and recreation.[^16]

Bengali culture is not a sealed majority tradition. It has continuously borrowed from and exchanged music, food, vocabulary, weaving, agricultural knowledge, and ritual forms with neighboring Indigenous communities; Banglapedia explicitly notes long interaction between Bengali folk culture and Santal, Garo, Hajong, Chakma, Manipuri, Tripura, and Marma traditions.[^18]

## Chakma culture

### Homeland and identity

The Chakma, who also identify as Changma, are Bangladeshâ€™s largest officially enumerated Indigenous ethnic community. They are concentrated in the central and northern Chittagong Hill Tracts, especially Rangamati and Khagrachhari, and maintain transborder communities in India and Myanmar. Their cultural geography historically combined settled villages, river valleys, bamboo forests, and hill slopes used for shifting cultivation.[^19][^20]

### Language and literature

Chakma is an Indo-Aryan language, despite the communityâ€™s location among many Sino-Tibetan-speaking peoples. It has its own script, though Bangla script has often been used in everyday publication and schooling; bilingualism in Chakma and Bangla is common. Oral literature includes folktales, proverbs, songs, ritual speech, and the narrative art of *gengkhuli* singers, while modern periodicals and poetry have extended the written tradition.[^20][^19]

Language vitality varies by place. Urban education and Bangla-dominant institutions can weaken literacy in Chakma script even where spoken Chakma remains strong. In 2026, Chakma was one of five Indigenous languages included in the governmentâ€™s mother-tongue textbook and teacher-training program.[^21]

### Religion and worldview

Most Chakmas practice Theravada Buddhism, forming Bangladeshâ€™s largest Buddhist ethnic population. Monasteries, monks, Buddha Purnima, Kathina robe offering, prayer, merit-making, and funeral rites structure religious life, while older ideas concerning natural powers and protective beings continue in some settings. This layered practice is better described as locally embedded Buddhism than as a simple opposition between Buddhism and â€œanimism.â€[^19][^20]

### Social organization and governance

Chakma society is organized through families, homesteads, hamlets, clans and subclans. Descent and inheritance have historically been patrilineal, and marriage within the same prohibited kin segment is avoided; arranged marriage has been common, but love marriage, divorce, and widow remarriage are accepted under customary procedures. Village-level leadership involves the *karbari*, with *mouza* headmen and the Chakma Circle chief forming part of the CHTâ€™s layered customary-administrative system.[^22][^23]

This system exists alongside state administration and institutions created or strengthened under the 1997 CHT Accord. The Accord recognized the CHTâ€™s special character and provided for regional and hill-district councils, customary institutions, land-dispute resolution, and restrictions on land transfer, though implementation remains disputed.[^24][^3]

### Livelihood and material culture

Historically, Chakma households combined *jhum* shifting cultivation with wet-rice cultivation in valleys, fishing, forest collection, horticulture, and craft production. Rice, cotton, vegetables, roots, spices, and bamboo were central; bamboo supplied house frames, tools, containers, fencing, instruments, and craft material. Today, occupations include farming, public service, education, business, wage employment, weaving, and urban professions.[^19]

Weaving is a major marker of Chakma identity and womenâ€™s expertise. The traditional loom or *baen* is largely made from bamboo; pattern knowledge can be preserved in an *alam*, a cloth serving as a design record. Womenâ€™s principal garments include the lower-body *pinon* and upper cloth *hadi/khadi*, often featuring bright geometric borders; contemporary wear may combine these with blouses and other national styles.[^25][^26]

### Food, festival, and performance

Rice, fish, vegetables, bamboo shoots, meat, and fermented or dried ingredients form the core cuisine. Cooking in bamboo tubes or banana leaves demonstrates the adaptation of food technology to the hill environment. The celebrated mixed-vegetable dish *pachan* is strongly associated with the New Year festival.[^27]

*Biju* is the central annual cultural festival and one component of the broader CHT New Year complex commonly called Baisabi/Vaisabi. Its three phases are Phool Biju, Mul Biju, and Gojjya Pojjya: flowers and water mark purification and respect; households cook *pachan*, sweets, rice products, fish and meat; people visit one another; and elders receive ceremonial respect. Buddhist worship, household illumination, games, music, and community gathering join ecological renewal to moral renewal.[^28][^27]

Chakma music and dance appear at Buddhist fairs, harvest occasions, and secular festivities. The *Cheralam* bamboo dance coordinates dancersâ€™ steps between rhythmically moving bamboo poles, demanding precision and collective timing.[^29]

### Contemporary concerns

Land insecurity, displacement, incomplete implementation of the CHT Accord, reduced access to forests, urban migration, and Bangla-dominant education all affect cultural transmission. At the same time, Chakma writers, artists, monks, women weavers, student organizations, digital-content creators, and mother-tongue educators actively renew the language and cultural repertoire. Weaving has also become a market livelihood through which women sustain households while preserving culturally meaningful garments.[^30]

## Marma culture

### Homeland and historical orientation

The Marma live primarily in Bandarban, Rangamati, and Khagrachhari, with smaller communities in coastal districts. Their language and many cultural forms are historically connected to Arakan/Rakhine and broader Burmese traditions. â€œMarmaâ€ and â€œRakhineâ€ should not automatically be treated as interchangeable in Bangladesh: the communities have related languages and traditions but distinct historical identities and settlement experiences.[^31]

### Language and religion

Marma belongs to the Burmeseâ€“Rakhine area of the Sino-Tibetan language family and is traditionally written in a Burmese-derived script. Bangla is widely used in education, government, and inter-community communication, creating multilingual repertoires but also pressure on intergenerational Marma literacy.[^31]

Theravada Buddhism is central. Monasteries, monks, Buddha images, calendrical observances, merit-making, funerals, and festivals organize collective life, while beliefs concerning local deities, spirits, dreams, and protective rites remain part of lived religion. Religious and village institutions therefore overlap with ecological and kin-based obligations.[^32][^31]

### Society and governance

The nuclear household is common, but strong consanguineal and affinal kin ties connect households. Older descriptions record inheritance for both sons and daughters and significant economic authority for women, even where the husband is treated as formal household head. Monogamy predominates; customary accounts also document cross-cousin marriage, divorce rights for both spouses, and adjudication by village or *mouza* authorities.[^31]

Traditional governance has three linked levels: a village *karbari*, a *mouza* headman, and a circle chief. The Bohmong Circle and Mong Circle are especially important in Marma-populated areas. These institutions mediate custom, revenue, land, and dispute settlement while interacting with district and national administration.[^32][^31]

### Economy, settlement, and craft

Marma livelihoods historically centered on *jhum* cultivation, supplemented by homestead gardens, forest gathering, wage labor, basketry, brewing, and trade. Houses were commonly built from bamboo, wood, grass, and thatch on raised platforms, with storage, weaving, fuel, or animals accommodated beneath. Concrete houses and market-linked employment have expanded, but raised bamboo architecture remains an important cultural model.[^31]

Womenâ€™s weaving produces household cloth and distinctive garments. Marma women traditionally wear a wraparound *thami* with an *angi* upper garment; men wear variants of the sarong or lungi and jacket-like upper clothing. Bamboo, cane, wood, and earthenware feature heavily in domestic technology.[^31]

### Cuisine and Sangrai

Rice and boiled or lightly prepared vegetables are staples, with fish, meat, bamboo shoots, and the fermented or dried-fish paste *nappi* providing characteristic flavors. Home-brewed rice alcohol has had ceremonial and social roles, though practice varies with household preference and religious interpretation.[^31]

*Sangrai* is the Marma New Year, synchronized with the April New Year season and the wider Baisabi complex. Activities include cleaning and decorating homes, monastery visits, bathing Buddha images, community meetings, food preparation, games and dance; water pouring or splashing symbolizes purification and the washing away of the old year. Buddha Purnima, Prabarana Purnima, and Kathina robe offering are other major observances.[^33][^32]

### Contemporary change

Schooling, tourism, market demand, migration, and mass-produced clothing have altered language use, settlement, and craft economics. Cultural preservation depends not simply on staging festivals but on maintaining access to land, functioning monasteries and customary institutions, transmission of script and oral history, and fair returns for womenâ€™s weaving and other cultural labor.

## Santal culture

### Distribution and history

Santals are the largest Indigenous community of Bangladeshâ€™s plains and live mainly in the northwestern districts of Rajshahi and Rangpur divisions. Their wider homeland extends across Jharkhand, West Bengal, Odisha, Bihar, Assam, Nepal, and Bangladesh, making Santal culture inherently transborder. In Bangladesh, many Santal communities have long histories as cultivators and agricultural laborers.[^34]

The 1855â€“56 Santal Hul or rebellion, centered in colonial India, remains a major symbol of resistance to landlords, moneylenders, police, and extractive colonial institutions. The movement grew from land alienation and oppressive revenue and credit systems and has enduring significance in Santal political memory.[^35]

### Language and oral knowledge

Santali belongs to the Munda branch of the Austroasiatic language family. In Bangladesh, bilingual or multilingual use of Santali, Bangla, and sometimes Sadri is common. The Ol Chiki script, created by Raghunath Murmu in the twentieth century, has become an important emblem of Santali literary identity, although Bangla, Roman, Devanagari, and other scripts may also appear across the transborder community.[^36][^34]

Oral genres include origin narratives, clan histories, ritual songs, work songs, riddles, proverbs, folktales, and dance songs. Performance is not separate from social organization: knowledge is transmitted through repeated participation in agricultural work, festivals, marriage, and village gatherings.

### Clan, village, and family

Santal society is patrilineal and organized into exogamous clans, often associated in historical literature with animal, plant, or other totemic names. Same-clan marriage is prohibited because clan members are treated as kin. Women contribute centrally to cultivation, wage labor, brewing, food preparation, song, dance, and ritual life even where formal village offices are male-dominated.[^37][^36]

The village council, often called the Manjhi council, integrates civil, moral, and ritual authority. Its functionaries include the *manjhi* or headman, *paranik* deputy, *jog manjhi*, messenger or *godet*, and priest or *naeke*, with local variation in titles and responsibilities. The council settles disputes, coordinates festivals and collective labor, and maintains relations among families and clans.[^38]

Marriage is called *bapla* and has several recognized forms. Parentally negotiated marriage has been common, but love marriage and other customary pathways also exist; monogamy is usual, and clan exogamy is fundamental.[^39][^38]

### Religion and sacred ecology

Traditional Santal religion recognizes a supreme creator and a world of *bonga*â€”powers or beings associated with place, ancestry, protection, danger, and social life. Marang Buru and Jaher Era are major sacred figures in many communities, while the sacred grove or *jaher than* provides a ritual center. Christianity has grown significantly, and some families engage both Christian and inherited cultural forms; local religious patterns therefore vary.[^40][^34]

### Agriculture, food, and housing

Santal livelihoods have historically combined settled agriculture, forest use, livestock, fishing, gathering, and wage labor. Land loss and insecure tenancy have pushed many families toward seasonal agricultural labor and other low-paid work. Rice is the staple, eaten with vegetables, fish, pulses, leafy greens, and locally available meat. *Haria/hadia*, a fermented rice drink, has important roles in hospitality, ritual, and festival, though its use varies by denomination and household.[^34]

Villages traditionally consist of compact rows of mud-walled houses with swept courtyards. Wall painting and earthen decoration can accompany festivals and household renewal, especially around Sohrai in the wider Santal cultural region.

### Festival, dance, and music

Santal festivals follow the agricultural and flowering cycle. *Baha* welcomes spring blossoms; *Sohrai* celebrates harvest, cattle, household renewal, and community abundance; *Karam* centers on the Karam tree and communal wellbeing. Dates and ritual details vary locally.[^41][^34]

Dance is participatory and collective rather than primarily theatrical. Women and men commonly form linked lines or arcs, moving to drums, flutes, and cymbals; song lyrics hold history, courtship, humor, ecological observation, and moral commentary. Jhumur song and dance are especially associated with Karam and the western Bengalâ€“northwestern Bangladesh cultural zone.[^41]

### Contemporary concerns

Land alienation, poverty, discrimination, conversion-related divisions, and weak mother-tongue schooling threaten cultural continuity. Santali is nevertheless one of five languages in Bangladeshâ€™s expanded 2026 mother-tongue textbook program. Cultural security requires more than festival visibility: it depends on land, school materials in locally accepted scripts, trained teachers, protection of sacred groves, and support for community-controlled documentation.[^21]

## Garo or Mandi culture

### Names and homeland

People widely known as Garo in official and external usage commonly identify in Bangladesh as **Mandi** or **Mande**, â€œhuman/person,â€ while Aâ€™chik is another important self-designation in the wider transborder population. Major Bangladesh settlements lie in Mymensingh, Sherpur, Netrokona, Tangailâ€™s Madhupur tract, Gazipur, and adjoining border districts.[^42][^43][^44]

### Language

Mandi/Aâ€™chik belongs to the Sino-Tibetan language family and has multiple regional varieties, including Abeng, Atong, Ruga and others. Bangladeshi Mandi speakers are commonly bilingual in Bangla; Roman orthography is widely used in literacy and church settings, although the languageâ€™s heritage was predominantly oral. Younger-generation language shift is a growing concern, especially outside compact rural communities.[^45][^46]

### Matrilineal social organization

Mandi society is matrilineal: clan identity and descent pass through the mother, children take the maternal clan name, and customary property typically moves through women. **Matrilineal does not necessarily mean matriarchal.** Men can exercise household, village, church, and political authority even while lineage and inheritance follow women; field research has found formal father-headed households coexisting with maternal surnames and descent.[^42][^45]

Major clan names include Sangma, Marak, Momin, Shira, and Areng. Marriage within the same matrilineal clan is prohibited. Residence and property practices have changed with Christianity, wage employment, statutory law, and interaction with Bengali society, and some families now distribute property to sons as well as daughters.[^44][^42]

### Religion

The inherited Mandi religious system is generally called **Sangsarek**, integrating ancestors, agricultural rites, village protection, and a spirit-filled natural world. Most Bangladeshi Mandi are now Christianâ€”especially Roman Catholic or Protestantâ€”yet inherited songs, clan norms, foodways, and harvest observances continue to shape ethnic identity. Christianity also brought Roman literacy, schools, church music, and new marriage and funeral forms.[^40][^42]

### Livelihood, food, and dress

Mandi livelihoods vary by ecology: wet-rice farming, forest and hill agriculture, pineapple and banana cultivation in Madhupur, wage labor, teaching, nursing, urban service work, and small business. Rice is the staple. Pork, fish, dried fish, bamboo shoots, mushrooms, vegetables, and meat-and-vegetable preparations are culturally prominent; home-distilled drink historically accompanied hospitality and festivals, though Christian practice has changed its use in many families.[^42]

Modern clothing often follows wider Bangladeshi styles, while festival attire preserves woven wrap garments and patterned cloth. Dress terminology and designs differ by subgroup and location; fixed â€œnational costumeâ€ descriptions can obscure this variation.

### Wangala and expressive culture

*Wangala* or *Wanna* is the major harvest festival, traditionally expressing thanks for crops and marking the agricultural cycle through feasting, drumming, horn blowing, song, and dance. In urban Dhaka and other migrant settings, revived Wangala celebrations function not only as harvest ritual but as conscious public affirmation of Mandi identity.[^47][^48]

Oral traditions include myths, proverbs, genealogies, agricultural knowledge, and songs. Drums and buffalo-horn instruments historically organized collective dance, while church choirs and modern popular music now coexist with inherited forms.

### Contemporary concerns

Forest enclosure, land disputes, urban migration, Bangla-language schooling, and the decline of Mandi in younger domains affect the community. Mother-tongue education has received state support, but language maintenance still depends heavily on family use, especially mothersâ€™ transmission to children.[^46][^21]

## Khasi culture

### Settlement and language

Bangladeshâ€™s Khasi communities live mainly in hill and forest-edge settlements of Greater Sylhet, especially Moulvibazar, Sylhet, Habiganj, and Sunamganj. Their villages are commonly called **punjis**, social and territorial communities generally led by a *mantri* or local head. Khasi belongs to the Austroasiatic language family and is therefore linguistically distinct from neighboring Indo-Aryan Bangla and Sino-Tibetan languages.[^49][^50]

Roman script is used for Khasi literacy, aided historically by missionary education. Bangla is essential for markets, administration, and schooling, and English may be important in church and education. Smaller Bangladeshi speech communities can face particular difficulty obtaining locally appropriate school materials.

### Matriliny and family

Khasi society is matrilineal and often matrilocal: children belong to the motherâ€™s clan, and customary inheritance follows the female line. In the wider Khasi system, the youngest daughter often holds a special custodial role for ancestral property and family ritual, although actual Bangladesh practice varies by punji, denomination, and household.[^51][^52]

As with the Mandi, matriliny should not be equated automatically with female rule in every institution. Men may lead punji administration, external negotiation, religious organizations, and production, while women anchor descent, property continuity, household economy, and kin care.

### Religion and ethics

Many Bangladesh Khasi are Christian, especially Presbyterian or Catholic, while inherited Khasi religion and ecological beliefs continue to influence concepts of ancestry, purity, land, and moral conduct. Churches organize schooling, calendars, music, youth groups, and welfare, but clan and punji remain fundamental social units.[^50][^51]

### Betel-leaf agroforestry

Khasi livelihood and cultural ecology are strongly associated with tree-based betel-leaf cultivation. Vines climb living support trees around punjis, producing a multistoried agroforestry system rather than the trellised *baroj* common in the plains. Management requires detailed knowledge of shade, humidity, support-tree selection, pruning, disease, soil moisture, harvesting, sorting, and market timing.[^53][^49]

Research in Moulvibazar and Jaintapur found that betel leaf supplied most household cash income in studied settlements and that the gardens retained diverse timber, fruit, medicinal, spice, and bamboo species. These findings should not be generalized mechanically to every punji, but they show that culture, livelihood, and biodiversity management are tightly linked.[^54][^55]

Labor is often gender-organized: men tend vines and harvest at height, while women sort, bundle, market, and manage household finances, though actual divisions vary. Rice and market-purchased foods complement garden products; betel nut, fruit, vegetables, forest foods, fish, and meat feature in local diets.

### Material and expressive culture

Traditional houses use bamboo, timber, thatch, or corrugated materials and are arranged according to slope, garden access, clan relations, and water. Weaving, baskets, music, dance, oral narratives, and Christian choral performance remain culturally important. Community festivals combine church calendars with ethnic gatherings, New Year observance, food, dress, and public dance.

### Contemporary concerns

Many punjis lack secure title to the forests and gardens they have managed for generations. Tea-estate expansion, stone quarrying, deforestation, tourism, land conflict, plant disease, reduced rainfall, and market dependence can simultaneously damage income and cultural continuity. Safeguarding Khasi culture therefore requires tenure security and ecological protection, not only documentation of songs or dress.[^56][^57]

## Manipuri or Meitei culture

### Community and migration

â€œManipuriâ€ in Bangladesh can refer broadly to communities originating from Manipur, including Meitei and, in some contexts, Bishnupriya and Manipuri Muslim identities. This profile centers on the **Meitei** specified in the request. Meitei migration into present-day Bangladesh occurred in several periods associated with warfare and political upheaval; today the largest settlements are in Greater Sylhet, especially Kamalganj, Sreemangal, Kulaura, Barlekha, and parts of Habiganj and Sunamganj.[^58]

### Language and literature

Meitei Lon or Manipuri is a Sino-Tibetan language with a major literary history. Meitei Mayek is its historic and revived script, while Bengali script has also been used extensively. Bangladesh-based writers formed organizations and periodicals to sustain a local literary sphere, including the Bangladesh Manipuri Sahitya Sangsad.[^58]

Language transmission is under pressure from Bangla-dominant schooling and small, dispersed settlement. Ritual, song, theatre, community publication, and digital communication consequently carry special importance for maintaining vocabulary and expressive styles.

### Religion and ritual

Most Bangladeshi Meitei follow Gaudiya Vaishnavism associated with Chaitanya, while inherited Meitei deities and ritual concepts remain culturally significant. A smaller Manipuri Muslim community is known as Meitei Pangon/Pangal. Sankirtanaâ€”devotional singing, percussion, and movementâ€”marks worship and major life-cycle ceremonies from infancy through marriage and death.[^59][^58]

### Dance, theatre, and festival

Dance and music are not peripheral entertainment but core religious and social media. Manipuri dance emphasizes controlled, rounded movement, rhythmic subtlety, devotional narrative, and distinctive costume. Major genres include Lai Haraoba, Khamba-Thoibi, Maibi Jagoi and other inherited forms, alongside classical Rasa, Gostha Lila, and drum-centered performance.[^60]

Rasa Lila dramatizes Radhaâ€“Krishna devotion and is a major public festival in Bangladesh, especially at Madhabpur in Kamalganj. Historical accounts associate its Manipuri court formulation with Maharaja Bhagyachandra in the eighteenth century; the Bangladesh festival draws both devotees and wider audiences. Holi/Yaoshang, Janmashtami, Lai Haraoba, and community-specific ritual calendars further link performance with religion and season.[^61]

### Weaving and dress

Handloom weaving is one of the most important forms of Meitei womenâ€™s cultural labor. Cloth is produced for domestic use, ritual gifts, festival attire, and sale; designs encode historical and community meaning rather than serving merely as decoration. The *phanek* lower garment, shawls, saris, bedding, bags, and ceremonial fabrics are major products.[^62][^63]

Weaving also appears within performance: *Phisarol Jagoi* represents stages of cotton cultivation and cloth production, turning technical knowledge into a danced offering and a medium of intergenerational teaching. Factory-made goods marketed as â€œManipuriâ€ can undercut authentic weaving and detach motifs from their cultural context.[^63][^62]

### Food, household, and economy

Agriculture and weaving have historically been leading occupations, with trade, services, education, and salaried employment expanding. Rice, vegetables, leafy herbs, fish, fermented fish, pulses, and distinctively seasoned broths and salads characterize Meitei food. Preparation often emphasizes herbs, vegetables, and fermented flavors rather than heavy oil, although Bangladesh-local ingredients and tastes have influenced contemporary cuisine.[^63][^60]

Family and neighborhood associations organize ritual assistance, marriage, funeral duties, temple activities, dance training, and collective labor. Womenâ€™s weaving income, menâ€™s and womenâ€™s agricultural work, and community cultural organizations all sustain identity.

### Contemporary concerns

The principal pressures are language shift, reduced space for loom work, imitation products, limited institutional support, and the transformation of devotional performance into decontextualized stage spectacle. Strong safeguarding would support community-led language education, apprenticeships with recognized weavers and performers, geographic/authenticity labeling for textiles, and archives controlled by Manipuri institutions.

## Mro or Mru culture

### Homeland and names

The Mroâ€”also spelled Mru, Mraw, or historically Murongâ€”live mainly in remote parts of Bandarban along the Sangu and Matamuhuri river systems. Many identify themselves as **Mro/Marucha**, with a term for â€œhuman/personâ€ at the root of some self-designations. Settlement remoteness has helped preserve distinctive practices but has also limited access to schools, healthcare, markets, and political representation.[^64][^65]

### Language and script

Mro is a Sino-Tibetan language. It was historically transmitted orally; in the 1980s, Menlay/Manley Mro created a distinctive alphabet in association with the Krama religious movement. Primary materials have since been produced in the script, making literacy both an educational tool and a visible marker of Mro cultural self-renewal.[^66]

Bangla and neighboring hill languages are increasingly important for school, administration, and trade. The absence of adequately trained Mro-language teachers and the small scale of publishing constrain literacy even where the script is symbolically strong.

### Religion and cosmology

Inherited Mro religion recognizes powers associated with creation, hills, rivers, disease, cultivation, and village wellbeing. Banglapedia records Turai as creator, Sangtung as connected to hills and harvest purity, and Oreng as connected to rivers and communal protection. Ritual life historically lacked centralized temples, priesthood, and scripture, relying instead on household or village specialists, elders, myth, and performance.[^64]

Buddhist influence is present, some Mro are Christian or Muslim, and the twentieth-century **Krama** movement created a new religious and ethical system with its own script and teachings. Present-day Mro religious identity is therefore plural rather than uniformly â€œanimist.â€[^65][^64]

### Society and livelihood

Mro society has generally been described as patrilineal or patriarchal at household level, but women perform indispensable agricultural, household, weaving, and market labor. Villages consist of kin-linked households in elevated bamboo-and-timber houses adapted to steep terrain, ventilation, rainfall, storage, and livestock needs.[^64]

*Jhum* cultivation is the principal traditional livelihood, supplemented by horticulture, forest-food collection, livestock, hunting in older practice, wage labor, and market exchange. Rice, maize, sesame, chilli, cotton, vegetables, tubers, and fruit can be grown in mixed hill fields. Forest knowledge encompasses edible plants, medicinal species, construction materials, fuel, fibers, and seasonal indicators.[^67][^68]

### Dress, music, and ceremony

Mro clothing is handwoven and adapted to heat and hill labor, with distinctive wrap garments, bags, ornaments, beads, and headwear used especially in ceremony. The *plung/plong* mouth organ or flute complex is a highly visible symbol of Mro music and youth performance.

Ear-piercing or *Raiksharam/Roikkaram* is a major social rite that formally incorporates children into community identity and is accompanied by feasting. Post-harvest festivals include dancing, new-crop cakes, music, and offerings; terminology and practice vary among local groups.[^65][^64]

Older sources describe a ceremonial bull/cow sacrifice linked to a foundational myth explaining the loss of an original sacred book. The event has been represented publicly as the defining Mro custom, but contemporary Mro culture should not be reduced to a single dramatic ritual; religious change, animal-welfare concerns, cost, and community preference affect whether and how it is practiced.[^69][^64]

### Contemporary concerns

The Mro face land pressure, tourism and development projects, environmental degradation, administrative misnaming, poverty, and restricted service access. These pressures are cultural because jhum landscapes, sacred places, house forms, food systems, and oral knowledge depend on territorial continuity. Mro-led schooling, script publishing, community museums and archives, land protection, and participation in development decisions are therefore mutually reinforcing safeguards.[^70][^65]

## Tripura or Tripuri culture

### Homeland and identity

Tripuri communities connect Bangladeshâ€™s CHT and eastern plains with the historical kingdom and present Indian state of Tripura. In Bangladesh they live especially in Khagrachhari and other CHT districts, with settlements in Chattogram, Cumilla, Feni/Noakhali areas, Moulvibazar, and elsewhere. The community contains numerous *dofa* or subgroup identities, so â€œTripuraâ€ is an umbrella as well as a specific ethnocultural name.[^71]

### Language

Kokborok, literally associated with the peopleâ€™s speech, belongs to the Bodo-Garo branch of Sino-Tibetan. Bangladesh varieties differ by subgroup and locality, and Bangla bilingualism is widespread. Script choiceâ€”Roman or Bengaliâ€”is linked to education, denomination, regional contact, and language politics.[^72][^71]

In 2026, Tripura/Kokborok remained one of the five Indigenous languages included in Bangladeshâ€™s mother-tongue primary program. Effective education nevertheless requires teachers fluent in local varieties and orthographies accepted by communities.[^21]

### Society and religion

Tripuri society has traditionally been patrilineal, clan organized, and village based. Same-clan marriage restrictions, collective work, village leadership, and ceremonial exchange organize community life, although local practices differ across subgroups.[^71]

Most Bangladesh Tripuri are Hindu, often combining Vaishnava or Shakta forms with inherited deities and household or agricultural rites; Christian Tripuri communities are also significant in some localities. Garia, Ker, and other deity-centered observances in the wider Tripuri world express fertility, protection, and agricultural wellbeing.[^73][^71]

### Agriculture, craft, and food

Jhum cultivation historically shaped labor, calendar, ritual, food, and settlement, with wet rice, orchards, wage work, service employment, and business increasingly important. Rice, vegetables, bamboo shoots, fish, meat, dried or fermented ingredients, and local beverages form a cuisine adapted to hill resources.

Tripuri women are accomplished backstrap-loom weavers. Womenâ€™s emblematic garments include the patterned *rignai* lower wrap and *risa* upper cloth; menâ€™s older dress includes *rikutu/rimtai*, upper cloth, and turban forms, with terminology varying by subgroup. Cloth patterns visibly signal community heritage and can also generate cash income.[^74][^72]

### Buisu/Baisu

The New Year and spring festival is called **Buisu, Baisu, or Boishu** in different transliterations and is the Tripuri component of the shared CHT Baisabi season. Houses are cleaned and decorated, elders and neighbors are visited, special foods are prepared, and groups sing and dance from household to household. The festival joins agricultural renewal, youth participation, hospitality, kinship, and public ethnic identity.[^27][^74]

Dance and song also accompany marriage and religious observances. Group dances, drums, bamboo instruments, and call-and-response songs transform collective labor and seasonal experience into performance.

### Contemporary concerns

Kokborok language shift, land loss, denomination-based cultural fragmentation, migration, and generic commercialization of â€œtribalâ€ textiles can weaken locally specific traditions. Community-led curricula, documentation of subgroup varieties, fair compensation for weavers, and recognition of customary land and governance are more durable interventions than festival display alone.

## Oraon or Kurukh culture

### Distribution and language

Oraon communities live mainly across northwestern Bangladeshâ€”Rajshahi, Naogaon, Dinajpur, Rangpur, Joypurhat, Thakurgaon and adjoining districtsâ€”with smaller settlements elsewhere, including tea-estate areas. **Kurukh** is their Dravidian mother tongue; Sadri and Bangla are also widely used. This Dravidian linguistic affiliation distinguishes Kurukh from neighboring Santali, which is Austroasiatic.[^75][^76]

Kurukh in Bangladesh is endangered in many localities. Older speakers may retain fluent oral traditions while younger people increasingly use Bangla at school and at home; recent reporting records villages where many youth cannot understand or speak Kurukh well. Claims that Kurukh simply â€œhas no scriptâ€ need qualification: several writing systems exist in the wider transborder community, but no single one has secure, universal institutional use in Bangladesh.[^77]

### Clan and family

Oraon society is patrilineal and organized into exogamous clans, including names such as Tirkey, Ekka, Kujur, Lakra, Minz, and others. Marriage within oneâ€™s clan is prohibited because clan members are treated as extended kin. Women contribute centrally to cultivation, wage labor, household production, brewing, song, dance, and ritual, even where inheritance and formal leadership follow the male line.[^78][^76]

### Religion and worldview

Inherited Oraon religion centers on **Dharmesh/DharmÃ©**, associated with the sun and creation, together with ancestors, village powers, and beings connected to nature, disease, fertility, and protection. Hindu influence is visible in some communities; Christianity has a substantial presence; and a smaller neo-Buddhist movement has also emerged. Oraon cultural practice can therefore cut across several formal religious identities.[^76][^79][^75]

### Agriculture, food, and hospitality

Settled cultivation and agricultural labor are principal occupations. Rice is the staple, accompanied by vegetables, pulses, fish, meat, leafy plants, and seasonal forest foods. Home-brewed rice drink traditionally expresses hospitality and accompanies festivals, marriage, and collective work, though denomination and individual choice affect present practice.[^75]

Tattooing was historically associated with adulthood, beauty, identity, and religious protection, especially among women, but is declining. Clothing now largely follows regional Bangladeshi patterns, with ethnic dress and ornaments more visible at festivals and cultural programs.[^75]

### Festival, music, and dance

Oraon festivals align with agriculture and sacred ecology. **Sarhul** celebrates spring and the flowering sal tree; **Karam** invokes purity, protection, fertility, community wellbeing, and a good harvest; **Sohrai** is associated with crops and cattle; **Fagua** marks the spring/Holi season. At Karam, a branch is ceremonially brought, installed, honored with fasting, song, story, and dance, and later immersed or returned.[^80][^76][^75]

Dance and music are essential social institutions. Instruments include drums such as *madal* and *dhol*, flutes, cymbals, clappers, and locally made idiophones; line or circle dances bind participants through synchronized movement. Songs transmit Kurukh vocabulary, clan memory, agricultural knowledge, courtship, and moral narratives.[^75]

### Contemporary concerns

Land dispossession, precarious agricultural labor, poverty, religious fragmentation, and severe language shift are major threats. Kurukh revitalization requires locally chosen orthography, recordings of elder speakers, childrenâ€™s materials, teacher training, public performance spaces, and land-based cultural continuity.[^81][^77]

## Hajong culture

### Homeland and economy

Hajong settlements lie along the northern border plains and foothills, especially Netrokonaâ€™s Durgapur and Kalmakanda, Mymensinghâ€™s Haluaghat and Dhobaura, Sherpurâ€™s Nalitabari, Jhenaigati and Sreebardi, and parts of Sunamganj. Unlike many hill communities associated historically with shifting cultivation, Hajongs have a strong tradition of settled wet-rice agriculture.[^82][^83]

Paddy, pulses, jute, mustard, vegetables, livestock, fishing, bamboo craft, and wage labor have supported livelihoods. Land and agricultural self-sufficiency are prominent in collective memory, while partition-era migration and later land pressure reduced the communityâ€™s demographic and economic base.

### Language

Hajong is generally classified today as an Indo-Aryan language with substantial Tibeto-Burman substrate and contact influence. It has been written with Assamese/Bengali-derived and Roman orthographies, while Bangla dominates formal education in Bangladesh. Many children therefore speak Bangla more readily than Hajong, making home and festival domains important for transmission.[^84][^82]

### Society and kinship

Hajong society is patrilineal and patriarchal in its customary inheritance system; Banglapedia records male-line property transmission and a named clan or *nikni* structure. Historical and comparative sources suggest earlier social features changed under sustained Hindu and Assamese-Bengali influence, illustrating that kinship systems are dynamic rather than timeless.[^82]

Marriage is usually monogamous and community-endogamous, with locally recognized arrangements, bridewealth/groom payments, divorce, and widow remarriage. Present practice varies by education, law, religion, and family preference.[^84]

### Religion

Hajong religion combines Hindu traditions with older community-specific deities, ritual specialists, household rites, and agricultural observances. Shakta and Vaishnava orientations coexist; Durga, Kamakhya, Shiva, Lakshmi, and local protective powers may receive worship. Daily incense or lamp offerings, vows, and womenâ€™s ritual songs connect household spirituality with seasonal cycles.[^82]

### Dress, food, and craft

The most distinctive womenâ€™s garment is the **pathin/pathani**, a handwoven wrap with horizontal bands and strong color contrast. Upper cloths include *parsa/argon* forms; men historically wore locally woven lower cloth, gamcha, dhoti, and ceremonial headwear. Womenâ€™s loom skill produces clothing, wedding textiles, towels, and decorative cloth.[^83][^82]

Rice is the staple, with fish, vegetables, lentils, sticky rice, and permitted meats. Many Hindu Hajongs avoid beef and buffalo meat; food rules differ by household and locality. Binni or glutinous rice has special value in festive preparations.[^82]

### Festivals and performance

Alongside widely shared Hindu festivals, Hajongs observe distinct community ceremonies such as **Deuli**, **Bastu Puja**, and locally named harvest or seasonal rites. Bangladeshâ€™s Cultural Heritage Inventory records Deuli among Hajong womenâ€™s cultural practices in Haluaghat, Netrokona, Kalmakanda, Durgapur, and Sunamganj, involving ritual, drumming, and traditional dress.[^85]

Womenâ€™s vow songs, group dances, narrative songs, and instruments such as dhol, khol, dotara, and cymbals connect religion, courtship, household prosperity, and village solidarity. Netrokonaâ€™s folk record also identifies Hajong rhymes, ritual verses, riddles, and *gahen* songs as important regional heritage.[^86]

### Contemporary concerns

The small census population, cross-border dispersal, Bangla-dominant education, loss of farmland, and limited publication in Hajong make language and ritual transmission vulnerable. Preservation is strongest when women weavers, singers, ritual practitioners, and farmers are treated as knowledge holders rather than merely performers for external audiences.

## Rakhine culture

### Migration and coastal homeland

Bangladeshâ€™s Rakhine trace major settlement waves to displacement from Arakan in the late eighteenth century. Communities became established in Coxâ€™s Bazar, Chattogram, Patuakhali, Barguna, Kuakata, and neighboring coastal areas under changing Burmese and British rule. The story of Kuakataâ€™s name is locally linked to Rakhine settlers digging wells for fresh water after arriving on the coast.[^87][^88]

Rakhine cultural history is related to, but not identical with, that of the Marma. Bangladesh Rakhine identity is especially shaped by coastal settlement, maritime and agricultural livelihoods, migration memory, and minority life among Bengali-speaking populations.

### Language and religion

Rakhine is closely related to Burmese and is written in a Burmese-derived script. Bangla is widely used for school, government, and trade, while language shift is acute in small coastal settlements. Script teaching through monasteries and community organizations is therefore central to heritage maintenance.[^87]

Most Rakhine practice Theravada Buddhism. Monasteries and monks organize worship, moral instruction, festivals, funerals, manuscript traditions, and community gathering. Major observances include Buddha Purnima, the rains retreat, Prabarana, Kathina robe offering, full-moon rites, and recitation ceremonies.[^87]

### Economy and settlement

Historically, farming and fishing were principal occupations, supplemented by trade, weaving, boatbuilding, carpentry, metalwork, shopkeeping, and other crafts. Coastal ecology shaped rice cultivation, fish and dried-fish processing, salt-related work, transport, and adaptation to cyclones and salinity.[^87]

Rakhine settlements characteristically centered on a monastery and clustered households. Traditional houses used timber and bamboo, sometimes raised above damp ground, although concrete and corrugated construction is now common.

### Weaving, dress, and food

Womenâ€™s weaving is both livelihood and social practice. Loom work produces wrap skirts, blouses, shawls, bags, monksâ€™ robes, and household textiles; learning and working together also transmit language, memory, design, and collective identity. Rakhine dress shares features with the wider Arakanâ€“Myanmar textile world while retaining local village patterns.[^89]

Rice, fish, dried fish, seafood, vegetables, chilli, fermented condiments, and coastal produce form the cuisine. Food, speaking Rakhine, Buddhist observance, weaving, and festival participation operate together as mutually reinforcing identity practices rather than isolated cultural â€œtraits.â€[^89]

### Festivals and arts

**Sanggreng/Sangrain**, the water festival and New Year observance, combines cleaning, monastery worship, water pouring, food, youth gathering, song, and dance. Other festivals follow the Buddhist lunar calendar, including Prabarana and Kathina. Ballads, storytelling, folk songs, dance, riddles, and dramatic social entertainment traditionally accompany weddings and public ceremonies.[^87]

### Contemporary concerns

Rakhine communities in coastal Bangladesh have experienced sharp demographic decline in many settlements, land loss, migration, climate exposure, reduced language transmission, and weakening weaving markets. Cultural expression can strengthen resilience, but poverty and marginalization can also interrupt apprenticeship and participation. Secure community land, coastal adaptation, monastery-based language teaching, womenâ€™s weaving cooperatives, and documentation under Rakhine control are key safeguards.[^89]

## Shared patterns and differences

### Language families

The twelve cultures demonstrate that Bangladesh is linguistically more diverse than a simple Bengali/non-Bengali division suggests.

- **Indo-Aryan:** Bangla, Chakma, and modern Hajong classification.
- **Sino-Tibetan:** Marma, Mandi/Aâ€™chik, Meitei, Mro, Kokborok, and Rakhine.
- **Austroasiatic:** Santali and Khasi.
- **Dravidian:** Kurukh.

Multilingualism is normal: a community language may be used at home and in ritual, Bangla in school and administration, English in higher education, and an additional regional language in trade or church. Language vitality therefore depends less on whether some speakers remain than on whether children use the language across home, play, literacy, worship, digital media, and schooling.

### Kinship systems

| Pattern | Communities | Important qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Matrilineal | Garo/Mandi and Khasi | Descent and inheritance follow women, but men may hold major household, religious, or political authority.[^45][^52] |
| Predominantly patrilineal | Chakma, Santal, Manipuri/Meitei, Mro, Tripura, Oraon, Hajong | Womenâ€™s labor and ritual authority can be substantial despite male-line descent. |
| Mixed or bilateral inheritance features in historical descriptions | Marma | Both sons and daughters may inherit, with local and legal change over time.[^31] |
| Majority Bengali systems | Usually patrilineal in customary practice, with variation by religion and statutory law | Urbanization and legal reform have diversified residence, inheritance management, and household structure. |

### Religion

Formal labels alone conceal layered practice. Chakma, Marma, and Rakhine are predominantly Theravada Buddhist; most Mandi and Khasi are Christian; most Tripura and Hajong follow forms of Hinduism; Meitei life is strongly Vaishnava; Santal, Oraon, and Mro communities include inherited religions alongside Christian, Hindu, Buddhist, Krama, or other affiliations. In nearly every case, ecology, ancestors, locality, and agricultural cycles continue to shape ritual even when a world religion supplies institutional identity.

### Ecology and livelihood

Culture is materially tied to landscape. CHT jhum systems integrate mixed cropping, fallow knowledge, bamboo, forest foods, and seasonal ritual; Khasi betel-leaf agroforestry depends on living support trees and humid forest conditions; northwestern Santal and Oraon festivals follow settled agriculture, sal trees, cattle, and harvest; Rakhine life is coastal and maritime; Bengali culture carries riverine, deltaic, and rice-farming signatures.

Consequently, land loss, forest enclosure, quarrying, plantation expansion, salinity, tourism, and development-induced displacement are also forms of cultural disruption. A dance can be recorded, but the ecological knowledge encoded in it cannot survive fully if communities lose access to the fields, trees, rivers, and sacred places that give it meaning.

### Textile knowledge

Womenâ€™s weaving is a major identity technology among Chakma, Marma, Manipuri, Tripura, Hajong, Rakhine, and Mro communities. Cloth records pattern systems, life-stage obligations, ritual gifting, gendered skill, aesthetic judgment, and household economy. Safeguarding should therefore address raw-material access, loom spaces, apprenticeships, intellectual-property concerns, authentic labeling, and fair market compensationâ€”not simply display garments in festivals.

## Historical and policy context

The CHTâ€™s history differs from that of plains communities. The 1997 CHT Accord ended a prolonged armed conflict and established a framework involving the Regional Council, Hill District Councils, customary institutions, land-dispute mechanisms, displaced-person provisions, and recognition of the regionâ€™s special characteristics. International IDEAâ€™s 2026 assessment states that implementation remains incomplete and that overlapping institutions continue to complicate land and governance.[^3][^24]

Plains communitiesâ€”Santal, Oraon, Mandi, Khasi, Hajong, Manipuri, and many othersâ€”do not have an equivalent regional accord or land commission. Their settlements often lie within private estates, reserve forests, borderlands, or fragmented agricultural zones, making land claims administratively diverse and politically less visible.

Mother-tongue education has expanded but remains limited. In 2026, the government reported textbooks and teacher training in Chakma, Marma, Tripura, Garo, and Santal, with 37 books and 450 teachers in training and a policy commitment to add more languages. The initiative is significant, but effective implementation requires community-approved scripts, dialect-sensitive materials, fluent teachers, regular classroom use, and routes from early primary education into later grades.[^21]

## Safeguarding priorities

### Community authority

Documentation should proceed with free, informed community participation. Communities should determine how sacred knowledge, funeral rites, medicinal knowledge, designs, and recordings may be accessed, attributed, or restricted. â€œOpen accessâ€ is not automatically ethical where outsiders can commercialize a motif, song, dataset, or ritual image.

### Language infrastructure

Each language needs a community-defined plan covering orthography, fonts and keyboards, childrenâ€™s books, dictionaries, grammars, audio-video corpora, teacher training, local radio, subtitles, and digital content. The most endangered cases in this group likely require urgent intergenerational recording and child-centered revitalization, especially Kurukh, Hajong, Rakhine, Mro, Khasi, and smaller local Manipuri varieties; even larger languages need domain expansion rather than mere speaker counts.

### Land and ecology

Cultural protection should include tenure security, access to forests and water, recognition of customary use, and environmental impact assessment that includes intangible heritage. Khasi betel gardens, Mro and other CHT jhum landscapes, Mandi forest settlements, Santal sacred groves, and Rakhine coastal villages are living cultural infrastructures.

### Living arts and livelihoods

Support should prioritize practitioners: women weavers, ritual experts, storytellers, musicians, farmers, midwives, language teachers, monks, priests, and community archivists. Fair-pay apprenticeship programs, cooperative marketing, authenticity labeling, and community-owned museums or digital repositories can join economic resilience with cultural transmission.

### Better research practice

Future research should avoid portraying cultures as static inventories of costume, food, and festivals. Strong studies should document internal variation, womenâ€™s and youth perspectives, denomination, urban and diasporic life, disability, class, multilingual practice, climate change, land tenure, and cultural innovation. Population figures, self-names, scripts, and festival terminology should be checked directly with relevant communities because official and older reference works often disagree.

## Conclusion

These twelve cultures reveal Bangladesh not as a culturally uniform state with peripheral â€œtribes,â€ but as a multilingual social landscape created through river deltas, coastal migration, hill agriculture, forest economies, border histories, religious movements, kingdoms, colonial rule, nationalism, and contemporary mobility. Bengali culture supplies the national majority framework, yet the Chakma, Marma, Santal, Mandi, Khasi, Meitei, Mro, Tripuri, Oraon, Hajong, and Rakhine sustain distinct systems of language, kinship, knowledge, art, ritual, and relation to land.

Their futures depend on whether culture is treated as a living right rather than a decorative performance. Mother-tongue education, secure land, ecological continuity, functioning customary institutions, fair livelihoods for cultural practitioners, and community control over documentation are the conditions under which festivals, scripts, weaving, oral literature, and ritual knowledge can remain meaningful across generations.
